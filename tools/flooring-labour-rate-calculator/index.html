        A worked example, step by step

        Made-up figures to show the method, not going rates: a fitter costs £220 a day and lays 40 m² in a full fitting day, but 10% of the paid day goes on travel and loading. Overheads are 15% and the target margin is 25%.

        1. Productive m² per paid day: 40 m² × (1 − 0.10) = 36 m²
        2. Labour cost per m²: £220 ÷ 36 m² = £6.11
        3. Add 15% overheads: £6.11 × 1.15 = £7.03 cost per m²
        4. Price for a 25% margin: £7.03 ÷ (1 − 0.25) = £9.37 per m² ex VAT
        5. If you're VAT registered: £9.37 × 1.2 = £11.24 per m² inc VAT

        Check it backwards on the second tab: 36 m² at £9.37 brings in £337.32 a day against £253 of labour and overheads, leaving £84.32. That's a 25% margin, or a 33.3% markup.

        Why work out your fitting rate from a real day cost

        A per m² rate copied from a competitor or carried over from years ago says nothing about your costs. Build it from what a fitting day costs your business, and every square metre you quote carries its share of wages, overheads and profit.

        It also shows where margin leaks. Every rate assumes a number of m² a day. When a job runs slower, the day costs the same, so the profit goes first. Knowing the day cost behind the rate lets you spot a slow job while you're pricing it, not after it's fitted.

        What goes into a fitter's day cost

        Employed fitters

        An employed fitter costs more than their wage: add employer's National Insurance, pension contributions, holiday and sick pay, and the van, fuel, insurance, tools and training that come with the job. Divide by the days they actually spend fitting, not the days you pay for. The yearly-figures helper does the sum.

        Subcontract fitters

        Check what a subcontractor's day rate includes, such as their own van, tools and fuel. You normally pay only for the days you book, but travel, waiting and return visits can still cost you. Paying subcontractors can also bring in the Construction Industry Scheme: see CIS for flooring contractors.

        Compare the two on cost per m² actually fitted, not on the headline day figure.

        m² per day changes with every job

        There's no honest single figure for how many square metres a fitter lays in a day. It moves with the product, the layout and the building. These all slow a day down:

        • Stairs, which are easier to price per step or per flight than per m²
        • Patterned carpet that has to match across seams
        • Herringbone, chevron, borders and feature strips
        • Sheet vinyl with welded seams and coved skirting
        • Uplifting and disposing of the old floor, and moving furniture
        • Subfloor preparation such as latex, smoothing compound or ply
        • Access: upper floors, parking, occupied homes and live sites

        So run the calculator for each floor type with its own m² per day, and price the slow work separately.

        Margin vs markup: know which one you're using

        Margin is profit as a share of the selling price. Markup is profit as a share of your cost. They're different numbers, and mixing them up quietly takes money off every job.

        Price = cost ÷ (1 − margin) Markup = (price − cost) ÷ cost Margin = (price − cost) ÷ price

        Take £100 of cost to keep the maths simple. Add a 25% markup and you charge £125, but your margin is only £25 ÷ £125 = 20%. For a true 25% margin you need £100 ÷ 0.75 = £133.33, which is a 33.3% markup.

        Charge extras as their own lines

        Your per m² fitting rate should pay for laying the floor. Everything else gets its own line, so it's visible, priced on purpose and easy to remove if the customer does it themselves:

        • Uplift and disposal of the old floor
        • Door bars and thresholds
        • Gripper and underlay
        • Preparation: latex or smoothing compound, ply and repairs
        • Furniture moving, and stairs per step or per flight
        • Return visits when a job is fitted in stages

        Work out materials with the carpet calculator, flooring pack calculator or smoothing compound calculator, and see how to quote a flooring job for the whole quote.

        Set a minimum charge

        Small jobs are where per m² pricing breaks down: a few square metres at your normal rate won't pay for loading the van, driving over and parking. Decide the smallest block of time a visit takes, such as half a day or a full day including travel, and use the minimum charge helper to add travel costs, overheads and margin. Then quote whichever is higher, the m² price or the minimum.

        VAT and your labour rate

        Work your rate out ex VAT, as the calculator does. If you're VAT registered, tick the box and it adds the standard rate of 20%. You have to be registered before you can charge VAT, so if you aren't, the ex-VAT price is what the customer pays.

        Not every job is standard-rated. Work for other contractors reported under the Construction Industry Scheme can fall under the VAT domestic reverse charge (see CIS for flooring contractors), and some building work, such as new homes and conversions, can be zero-rated or reduced-rated, as HMRC's VAT Notice 708 explains. If either applies, check with your accountant.
